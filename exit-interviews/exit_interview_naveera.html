2Reasons for Leaving

Three separate drivers, recorded in the order of importance she gave them — the decision to leave pre-dated the opportunity that made it possible.

This is not simply a "leaving to study" case. Dissatisfaction with the field technology rollout was an existing and independent factor, and the fatigue had been flagged before without resolution. Recording the exit as PhD-driven alone would obscure a genuine retention signal.

3What Worked — Strengths

  • Teamwork and atmosphere. A genuinely strong teamwork culture at the hub. Her biggest personal learning was collaboration and how to support others.
  • Technology team. "A big support, always." Her concerns were about product decisions, not the people delivering them.
  • Manager and colleagues. Support was consistently available; colleagues stepped in whenever a field issue arose.
  • Cover during medical leave. Coaches took on her workload voluntarily during her surgery and recovery. Nothing was left unfinished — her strongest example of the culture.
  • Trainings and summer workstreams. Every member delivered their share without needing follow-up.
Strength to protect

Peer support and voluntary workload cover are a demonstrated strength. They should be formally recognised rather than assumed.

4Issues Formally Flagged

4.1 Professionalism and professional ethics  Primary flag

Her strongest concern, raised twice — at organisational level and again when asked specifically about HR and the RM cadre.

  • Workplace language. An overall shortfall in professionalism, particularly in the language used in the office. She was clear that her concern is not ordinary humour, but remarks that go "over the line" — and that a number of people do this.
  • No defined boundary on what is and is not appropriate to discuss during office hours.
  • RMs discussing coaches with other coaches. Her most specific concern. She repeatedly heard information not meant for her — about another sector or another coach — shared with her directly by an RM. RM-to-RM discussion is understandable; an RM discussing a coach with a different, subordinate coach is unprofessional. The impact: it creates a pre-formed impression of that coach, and because the practice is widespread, "everyone ends up knowing something about everyone." She described the damage as unpredictable. Many coaches are also personal friends, so information received in one capacity distorts the relationship in another. She had already raised this with Bilal — this is not the first report.
Action she requested

A deliberate effort on professional ethics and communication boundaries, involving everyone including leadership and the RM cadre — not coaches alone.

4.2 Technology is burdening teachers rather than supporting them

She named the tools specifically: the chatbot, AI-based transcription and the digital lesson plans. In her own assessment these are adding to teachers' burden instead of facilitating their work.

  • She sees teachers visibly upset and distressed, particularly those receiving feedback on lesson-plan quality — so the concern is how quality judgements reach teachers, not the tools' existence.
  • In fairness, she accepted that some of this may streamline over time; her objection is to the present experience on the ground.
  • This is what produced her sense of being "in the wrong place, specifically with regard to teachers and students."
Why this matters most

This is a mission-alignment concern, not a product complaint. A coach with 2.5 years in the field is reporting that what we build is distressing the people the organisation exists to serve. Of everything raised, this carries the greatest risk.

4.3 The escalation route feels closed

  • She raised these concerns in multiple forums and directly with her RM, and described herself as someone who speaks up consistently.
  • The response she received was that these matters are not within the field team's control — decisions come from leadership, and the field can raise concerns but nothing more.
  • Her conclusion after 2.5 years: anything originating from the leadership mindset has to be implemented regardless of whether it benefits the field.
Action she requested

A credible feedback loop where field concerns visibly influence decisions instead of being acknowledged and closed. Her position amounts to learned helplessness — someone who spoke up for 2.5 years concluded that speaking up changed nothing.

4.4 Product testing is not conducted in representative schools

Her most concrete and most implementable recommendation.

  • She credited the organisation for having sent staff into the field to test. But from what she has heard, testing has largely been in schools that are nearby, better resourced and urban.
  • Conditions differ sharply where coaches actually work — the villages around Tarnol, where conditions are very poor, and the hilly, remote settlements with no internet facility at all.
  • Her recommendation: the teams building these tools should themselves visit the distant, low-resource schools, so they get a reality check and can design in a more grounded way. She did not ask for the tools to be withdrawn — she asked for the builders to be put on the ground.

7Overall Assessment

The separation is voluntary and amicable, with no performance or conduct concerns on either side; a rehire recommendation is appropriate. The exit is driven principally by a confirmed PhD admission in January and a need for rest — but dissatisfaction with the direction of the field technology rollout was an existing and independent factor, and should not be recorded as a study-related departure alone.

Her feedback carries weight because it is specific. She distinguishes clearly between what worked — people, collaboration, peer support, admin and operations — and what did not: professional boundaries, the burden the current tools place on teachers, and product testing that is not representative of the schools coaches actually serve. Action items 1 to 4 are the substantive takeaways. The interview closed with thanks for her service and best wishes for her doctoral studies.
